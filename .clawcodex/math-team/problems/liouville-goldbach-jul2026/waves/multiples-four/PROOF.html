Every positive multiple of four has a Liouville-negative representation

For a positive integer nn, let Ω(n)\Omega(n) be the number of prime factors of nn, counted with multiplicity, and put

λ(n)=(−1)Ω(n)∈ℤ. \lambda(n)=(-1)^{\Omega(n)}\in\mathbb Z.

In particular, Ω(1)=0\Omega(1)=0. Write HasRepresentation⁡(N)\operatorname{HasRepresentation}(N) for the assertion that there exist a,b∈ℕa,b\in\mathbb N such that

0<a,0<b,N=a+b,λ(a)=λ(b)=−1. 0<a,\qquad 0<b,\qquad N=a+b,\qquad \lambda(a)=\lambda(b)=-1.

Equal witnesses are allowed.

Theorem. For every m∈ℕm\in\mathbb N with m>0m>0,

HasRepresentation⁡(4m). \operatorname{HasRepresentation}(4m).

1. Elementary Liouville facts

Positive prime factorization gives, for all positive integers u,vu,v,

Ω(uv)=Ω(u)+Ω(v),λ(uv)=λ(u)λ(v).(1) \Omega(uv)=\Omega(u)+\Omega(v), \qquad \lambda(uv)=\lambda(u)\lambda(v). \tag{1}

No coprimality assumption is needed: multiplicities add even when the two factors have common prime divisors. Also,

λ(1)=1,λ(n)∈{1,−1}(n>0),λ(r)=−1(r prime).(2) \lambda(1)=1,\qquad \lambda(n)\in\{1,-1\}\quad(n>0), \qquad \lambda(r)=-1\quad(r\text{ prime}). \tag{2}

Consequently,

λ(2)=λ(3)=λ(5)=−1,λ(4)=1,λ(t2)=1(t>0).(3) \lambda(2)=\lambda(3)=\lambda(5)=-1, \qquad \lambda(4)=1, \qquad \lambda(t^2)=1\quad(t>0). \tag{3}

These are the positive-argument factorization identities; no value of λ(0)\lambda(0) will be used.

We also use two elementary consequences of prime factorization. A prime dividing a product divides at least one factor, so cancellation by a nonzero residue is valid modulo a prime. Every integer greater than 11 has a prime divisor: its least divisor greater than 11 must be prime, since a nontrivial proper factor of that divisor would be a smaller divisor greater than 11.

3. Consequences of a missing representation

For the next two sections, let pp be an odd prime with p≠3p\ne3, and let

4. Ternary gap descent and full antireflection

Call a pair (x,y)(x,y) a defect if

x>0,y>0,x+y=p,f(x)=f(y)=1. x>0,\qquad y>0,\qquad x+y=p,\qquad f(x)=f(y)=1.

In particular, both xx and yy lie strictly below pp. We prove that no defect exists.

4.1. Neither entry of a defect is divisible by three

Suppose x=3ux=3u in a defect. Then 0<u<p0<u<p, and (6) gives f(u)=−1f(u)=-1. By (A), f(p−u)=1f(p-u)=1, whence

f(3p−x)=f(3(p−u))=−1. f(3p-x)=f(3(p-u))=-1.

On the other hand, (C) applied to v=yv=y, where 0<y<p<2p0<y<p<2p, gives

These arguments are positive and have total 4p4p. More precisely,

2p<3p−x<3p,p<p+x<2p. 2p<3p-x<3p,\qquad p<p+x<2p.

This contradicts (5). Thus 3∤x3\nmid x; the same argument with x,yx,y exchanged gives 3∤y3\nmid y.

4.2. Exact division gives another defect

Because pp is prime and p≠3p\ne3, we also have 3∤p3\nmid p. The nonzero residues of x,yx,y modulo 33 must therefore agree: distinct nonzero residues would be 1,21,2, whose sum is 00 modulo 33. It follows that

Thus the divisions in

are exact. Both new integers are positive, and

x′+y′=2p+x+y3=p. x'+y'=\frac{2p+x+y}{3}=p.

By (C), applied to yy and xx respectively,

f(p+x)=f(p+y)=−1. f(p+x)=f(p+y)=-1.

Since p+x=3x′p+x=3x', p+y=3y′p+y=3y', and f(3)=−1f(3)=-1, complete multiplicativity gives

f(x′)=f(y′)=1. f(x')=f(y')=1.

Hence (x′,y′)(x',y') is again a defect. Its entries, being positive and summing to pp, are both strictly less than pp.

4.3. The positive integer gap decreases

As pp is odd, a defect cannot have x=yx=y. If any defect exists, exchange its entries to arrange x<yx<y, and choose one for which the positive integer gap

δ=y−x \delta=y-x

is minimal. Such a minimum exists, since the possible pairs form a finite set. Construction (8) preserves the order and gives

0<y′−x′=y−x3=δ3<δ. 0<y'-x'=\frac{y-x}{3}=\frac{\delta}{3}<\delta.

The new gap is an integer because both divisions in (8) have already been proved exact. We have obtained another defect with a smaller positive integer gap, a contradiction.

There are therefore no positive-positive sign pairs of total pp. There are also no negative-negative sign pairs of that total, by (A). Since the only signs are 1,−11,-1, we conclude that

5. Antireflection forces multiplicativity on nonzero residues

7. Representations for every odd prime

We apply the preceding arguments with f=λf=\lambda. Equations (1)–(3) give complete multiplicativity, the two sign values, and f(2)=f(p)=−1f(2)=f(p)=-1 whenever pp is prime.

7.1. Primes congruent to three modulo four

Let p≥7p\ge7 be prime with p≡3(mod4)p\equiv3\pmod4. Suppose HasRepresentation⁡(4p)\operatorname{HasRepresentation}(4p) fails. All hypotheses of §§3–5 hold, so a negative-sign nonzero square strictly below pp would contradict §5.3.

7.2. Primes congruent to one modulo four

Every residue in RpR_p has an inverse, as proved in §5. Pair each residue with its inverse. The self-inverse residues satisfy a2=1a^2=1, hence

Put h=(p−1)/2h=(p-1)/2. Pairing the integer representatives instead as j,p−jj,p-j, for j=1,…,hj=1,\ldots,h, gives

7.3. The prime three

For p=3p=3, take the positive witnesses

8. Conclusion for every positive multiplier

The odd-prime assertion (4), together with the reduction in §2, proves the theorem for every m>0m>0. Explicitly, the diagonal witnesses 2m,2m2m,2m handle λ(m)=1\lambda(m)=1; the positive scaled pairs from 8=3+5=4+48=3+5=4+4 handle even mm; and for odd mm with λ(m)=−1\lambda(m)=-1, a prime factorization m=pdm=pd with λ(d)=1\lambda(d)=1 scales a representation of 4p4p to one of 4m4m.

In every case the witnesses are positive natural numbers, their sum is exactly 4m4m, and each has Liouville value −1-1. No distinctness, parity, or coprimality condition is imposed on the witnesses. □\square
